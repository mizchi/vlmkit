Why we walk routes twice

The first walk is for the route itself: where the water is, which junctions mislead, whether the 4pm light makes the descent worth slowing down for. We take almost no notes. The route has to earn a second visit before it earns a page.

The second walk is the written one. Every guide in Atlas is the record of that second pass — distances re-measured, cafés re-tested on a weekday, the one confusing stile photographed from both sides. If a detail matters at 6am in the rain, it is in the guide.

Between the two walks, routes change. A landslide closes a traverse, a ferry timetable shifts, a farm dog retires. Readers file corrections from the trail, and each guide shows the date it was last walked, not just last edited.

Slow travel is not about pace for its own sake. It is about a ratio — hours moving to hours noticing. The guides are structured around that ratio: shorter days than you expect, longer lunches than you plan, and a firm opinion about which viewpoints justify a climb.
